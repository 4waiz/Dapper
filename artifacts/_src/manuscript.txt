DAPPER: Deadline-Aware Edge Perception for 
Mission-Critical Robots under Dynamic Network 
Conditions 
Abstract—Mission-critical robots require perception outputs 
that are not only accurate but also fresh enough for the control 
loop: a confident detection that arrives after the actuation deadline 
is operationally equivalent to a miss. Fixed offloading to a fog, 
edge, or cloud server can raise perception quality, yet it exposes 
the robot to latency growth, jitter, packet loss, server load, and 
outages. 
This 
paper 
presents 
DAPPER 
(Deadline-Aware 
Perception Placement for Edge Robotics), a deadline-aware 
perception-placement framework that treats execution-site 
selection as a per-frame runtime decision rather than a static 
offloading choice. From lightweight runtime estimates of round-
trip time, packet loss, edge load, deadline pressure, frame 
freshness, and local-model confidence, DAPPER computes an 
auditable deadline-risk score and selects one of four modes: local-
fast, edge-accurate, hybrid, or degraded-safe. We evaluate 
DAPPER in a controlled and reproducible benchmark of 10,000 
frame-level decisions spanning five network profiles and four 
execution policies. DAPPER sustained a 0.0% deadline-miss 
rate in every profile. Under congestion, fixed edge-only and cloud-
only policies missed 94.6% of deadlines. Under variable conditions, 
DAPPER held 95th-percentile latency at 33.6 ms versus 
166.3 ms for edge-only and 290.2 ms for cloud-only, while reducing 
offload bandwidth from 9,150 KB and 12,810 KB to 25 KB. The 
benchmark is synthetic rather than a physical robot deployment; 
its contribution is a reproducible prototype and empirical stress 
test for deadline-aware edge-perception scheduling.

I. INTRODUCTION 
obotic perception increasingly depends on com- 
putationally intensive algorithms for object detec- 
tion, scene understanding, tracking, navigation, and 
hazard recognition.  In mission-critical settings such as 
industrial inspection, emergency response, unmanned aerial 
systems, warehouse automation, and au-tonomous security 
patrols, a perception result is useful only if it arrives before 
the robot must act. A high-confidence output produced after the 
control deadline is, for the decision loop, operationally 
equivalent to a missed detection. Real-time robotic perception 
is therefore governed less by average accuracy than by timing: 
deadline compliance, tail latency, result freshness, and 
predictable fallback behaviour. 
Cloud computing offers abundant compute, but wide-area 
execution adds latency, jitter, intermittent disconnection, and 
privacy exposure. Fog and mobile edge computing reduce 
this distance by placing services near the access network, which 
matches their core motivation of bringing low-latency 
computation closer to connected devices. Yet proximity alone 
does not solve the problem: wireless links fade, nearby edge 
nodes become congested, server load fluctuates, and the best 
execution site changes from one frame to the next. A placement 
decision that is optimal when a robot enters a coverage cell can 
become a liability seconds later. 
We argue that mission-critical robotic perception should be 
modelled as a continuous deadline-management problem rather 
than a one-shot task-offloading problem. The robot should not 
bind itself statically to local or remote inference. Instead it 
should decide, per frame, whether to run a fast local model, 
offload to an edge server, combine the two, or retreat to a safe 
local path when network risk becomes unacceptable. It should 
also reject remote results that arrive too stale to trust. 
To this end we present DAPPER. The framework selects an 
execution mode every frame from runtime estimates of round-
trip time (RTT), packet loss, edge load, deadline pressure, frame 
freshness, and the confidence the local model would yield. It is 
built for perception loops in which freshness and deadline 
compliance are first-order requirements, and its policy is 
deliberately deterministic and auditable: each decision carries a 
numeric risk score and a human-readable reason. Our 
contributions are: 
• a problem framing that connects fog/cloud robotics, collab-
orative inference, and fog/edge scheduling to the specific 
demands of deadline-aware, freshness-aware mission-
critical perception; 
• DAPPER, a confidence- and deadline-aware perception-
placement architecture with local-fast, edge-accurate, 
hybrid, and degraded-safe modes, a safe local fallback, and 
freshness-gated acceptance of remote results; 
• a reproducible Python prototype comprising a deter-
ministic scheduler, a runtime monitor, local/edge/cloud 
perception simulators, a command-line benchmark runner, 
a metrics module, a plotting pipeline, and unit tests; and 
• a controlled evaluation over 10,000 frame-level decisions 
across five network profiles and four execution policies, 
showing that adaptive placement can preserve deadlines 
while sharply reducing offload bandwidth.

B. Collaborative DNN Inference 
Collaborative inference studies how neural-network compu-
tation can be split or adapted across device, edge, and cloud. 
Neurosurgeon showed that partitioning a DNN between mobile 
device and cloud can reduce latency and energy [4]; Edgent 
combined device-edge partitioning with early exits and right-
sizing for on-demand edge intelligence [5]. These methods 
chiefly optimise inference latency, energy, or accuracy. Generic 
partitioning, however, is not the same as robotic perception 
safety: the robotic loop additionally needs freshness constraints, 
explicit failover, recall of safety-critical objects, and middleware 
timing guarantees. DAPPER is orthogonal to model partitioning. 
It decides where and whether to run perception under a deadline, 
and it could sit above a partitioned model as its edge backend. 
C. Fog/Edge Scheduling 
Surveys of fog and edge scheduling stress heterogeneity, 
dynamic workloads, uncertain networks, and multi-objective 
observes network state (RTT, packet loss, edge load, and edge 
availability), an estimate of local-model confidence, the frame 
age at, and the age vt of the last valid output. From these signals, 
DAPPER selects one of four modes: 
• local-fast: lightweight on-device inference, with low 
predictable latency but lower confidence and no network 
dependence; 
• edge-accurate: remote edge or cloud inference, with higher 
confidence but network latency, chosen only when the 
round trip fits the deadline with margin; 
• hybrid: local output is returned immediately and an edge 
refresh is accepted only if it would arrive inside a freshness 
window and before the deadline; and 
• degraded-safe: reuse of the last valid output while it is 
fresh, or a guaranteed local inference, used when deadline 
risk is high or the edge is unreachable. 
The scheduler first computes a normalised deadline-risk score 
in [0, 1] as a weighted sum of five signals: normalised RTT, 
packet loss, edge load, frame age, and a deadline-pressure term 
equal to the estimated edge round trip divided by the deadline:

The target system contains a robot or camera-equipped client, 
a nearby fog/edge node, and an optional cloud service. The 
client captures frame ft at time t and must produce a perception 
output before a deadline D, the control-loop budget. The

The weights and thresholds are loaded from a configuration file, 
allowing reviewers to rerun ablations without changing code. 
Two thresholds map the score to behaviour: above the degraded 
threshold the scheduler chooses degraded-safe; above the local 
threshold it stays local-fast for predictable latency. In the 
remaining low-risk region, the choice is confidence- and margin-
aware. If the local model is already confident enough, DAPPER 
keeps the frame on device to save bandwidth and remove 
residual network risk. If the local result is weak, offloading 
is worthwhile: DAPPER picks pure edge-accurate when the 
estimated edge round trip consumes at most a configurable 
fraction of the deadline, and hybrid otherwise so that a fast local 
answer is always in hand. Every remote round trip is charged its 
configured access RTT and upload bandwidth, including 
attempts that are lost or arrive too late to be accepted. 
A.  Fog, Cloud, and Edge Robotics
Cloud and fog robotics show that robots benefit from remote
compute. FogROS2 provides an adaptive ROS 2 platform for
cloud and fog robotics and reports gains for heavy robotics
workloads when computation is offloaded [1]. FogROS2-PLR
studies probabilistic latency reliability and shows that
replicating network paths can reduce tail latency in
cloud-connected 
robotic 
experiments 
[2]. 
ElasticROS
investigates elastic collaboration between robots and remote
resources [3]. These systems solve essential infrastructure
problems: connecting robots to cloud and fog resources,
accelerating workloads, and managing latency at the system
level. The gap they leave is perception-loop awareness. A
perception scheduler must also decide when a result is too stale
to use, when a remote result should be rejected, and when a
degraded local path is safer than waiting. DAPPER targets this
layer and is complementary to such platforms.

IV. PROTOTYPE AND BENCHMARK METHODOLOGY 
A. Implementation 
The prototype is a self-contained Python project: a determin-
istic scheduler, a runtime network monitor, local/edge/cloud 
perception simulators, an optional FastAPI edge-inference 
service, a command-line benchmark runner, a metrics module, 
a plotting module, a configuration file, and unit tests for the 
scheduler and benchmark. It runs without a physical robot or 
any external model download, which makes the benchmark 
easy to reproduce but limits direct claims about real robot 
deployment. 
B. Baselines and Metrics 
Four policies are compared: local-only, edge-only, cloud-only, 
and DAPPER. The fixed baselines traverse the same benchmark 
path as DAPPER to preserve an apples-to-apples comparison; 
cloud-only differs from edge-only in its larger access RTT and 
compute envelope. Each remote path is charged a static access 
RTT on top of profile-driven jitter, and an offload attempt is 
billed its upload bandwidth even when the request is lost or 
returns too late to use. DAPPER invokes the scheduler at every 
frame; the baselines do not. 
Each run logs frame ID, profile, selected mode, RTT, loss, 
edge load, deadline, latency, deadline status, confidence proxy, 
stale-output and fallback flags, bandwidth, risk score, decision 
reason, and result source. The reported metrics are mean, 
p95, and p99 latency; deadline-miss rate; mean confidence 
proxy; stale-output rate; fallback rate; mode-switch count; and 
total/per-frame bandwidth. All runs use 500 frames per profile-
policy pair and a fixed seed of 42. The perception confidence 
values are simulated proxies, not detector accuracies. 
 
V. RESULTS 
Table III reports the full benchmark. DAPPER achieved 
a 0.0% deadline-miss rate in every profile, while the fixed 
offloading policies became progressively brittle as conditions 
degraded. Under congestion, edge-only and cloud-only each 
missed 94.6% of deadlines. Under variable conditions, edge-
only missed 68.0% and cloud-only missed 73.2%. Local-only 
never missed a deadline but is capped at the local confidence 
proxy and cannot exploit a healthy network.

C. Outage Behaviour 
Outage behaviour shows why fixed policies are unsafe for 
mission-critical perception. Edge-only and cloud-only suffered 
11.2% deadline misses with high tail latency: p95 of 304.7 ms 
and 418.4 ms, respectively. DAPPER instead entered degraded-
safe behaviour on 452 of 500 outage frames, producing 0.0% 
deadline misses with a 99.0% fallback rate. This high fallback 
rate is expected and desirable, not a failure: the scheduler 
recognised remote execution as unsafe and prioritised continuity 
of fresh, on-time output.

B. Latency and Bandwidth 
Adaptive placement also controls tail latency and bandwidth. 
Under the variable profile, DAPPER held p95 latency at 33.6 ms, 
against 166.3 ms for edge-only and 290.2 ms for cloud-only. 
It spent only 25 KB of offload bandwidth, compared with 9,150 
KB and 12,810 KB for fixed edge and cloud offloading. These 
savings arise because DAPPER offloads only when deadline 
risk is low and the local result is weak, and because it declines 
refreshes that would arrive stale. When conditions are clean but 
local confidence is low, DAPPER may spend bandwidth to 
fetch fresh edge results; bandwidth is therefore a controlled 
cost rather than a fixed tax. 
fixed offloading in a controlled fog/mobile-edge benchmark. 
Local-only is predictable but cannot exploit a healthy network; 
edge-only and cloud-only can raise the confidence proxy but are 
brittle under RTT growth, congestion, packet loss, and outage. 
DAPPER avoids this dichotomy by treating placement as a 
runtime decision, which is the kind of adaptive, locality-aware 
service placement that fog and mobile edge computing aim to 
enable for latency-sensitive applications. 
A second lesson is that the right evaluation target is 
not average latency alone. Tail latency, deadline-miss rate, 
freshness, fallback behaviour, and bandwidth matter more to a 
control loop. A policy that looks attractive on average can still 
fail during the transient bursts and outages that decide mission 
safety. 
The confidence results must be read with care. DAPPER does 
not maximise the confidence proxy: in several profiles it 
remains close to local-only and below fixed edge/cloud policies. 
This is an intentional trade-off. DAPPER spends its confidence 
budget only when a fresh, on-time remote result is achievable, 
and otherwise prefers a timely local answer. Whether that trade-
off is worthwhile depends on the application’s relative

VIII.  CONCLUSION 
We presented DAPPER, a deadline-aware, confidence-aware 
perception-placement framework for mission-critical robots. 
The prototype provides a reproducible benchmark with local, 
edge, cloud, and adaptive policies across stable, congested, 
lossy, variable, and outage profiles. Over 10,000 frame-level 
decisions, DAPPER achieved 0.0% deadline misses in every 
profile. Under congestion, the fixed edge and cloud policies

VII. THREATS TO VALIDITY 
The evaluation is controlled and synthetic. It is not a physical 
robot deployment, real wireless interference, or a production 
edge cluster. The confidence values are simulated proxies, not 
the accuracy of a trained detector such as YOLO; the local, 
edge, and cloud latencies are generated by prototype 
distributions rather than measured on Jetson-class hardware; and 
the network conditions are parameterised profiles rather than 
captured Wi-Fi, 5G, or private-edge traces. The deadline-
pressure term uses a fixed edge-compute estimate, and 
bandwidth is charged at a per-frame granularity that treats the 
upload as the dominant cost. 
These limitations restrict generalisation but do not undermine 
the controlled comparison, because every policy is evaluated 
under identical seeded conditions. The benchmark’s value is 
that it isolates scheduling behaviour and yields reproducible 
evidence. Future work should validate DAPPER on a physical 
missed 94.6% of deadlines while DAPPER stayed deadline-safe. 
Under variable conditions, DAPPER used 25 KB of offload 
bandwidth against 9,150 KB for edge-only and 12,810 KB for 
cloud-only, while holding p95 latency at local levels. 
The conclusion is intentionally modest: DAPPER is not a 
complete autonomous-robot deployment and does not prove 
real-world safety. It does show that deadline-aware, explainable 
placement with safe fallback and freshness-gated remote 
acceptance can improve reliability and bandwidth efficiency in 
controlled fog/mobile-edge robotic-perception benchmarks. 
The next step is hardware validation with real detectors and 
measured network traces.
